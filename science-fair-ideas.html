Winners briefing

Last year · winners briefing for 2026–27 teams

Keep the signal small. Make the human action clear.

The primary winners of last year’s Fair each took a problem people in Hong Kong already feel, read it with a simple board, and turned the reading into something a person does: pay, play, look up, or move. This page is for teams who are strong at interface design and who can vibe-code apps and games on micro:bit and Arduino.

  1. 01Name a person and a moment, then show you asked them.
  2. 02Read one physical signal the board can actually measure.
  3. 03Put the team’s design skill in the screen, the game, or the controls.
  4. 04Show two approaches you tried, dropped, and can explain.
  5. 05Bring three versions, each changed by a test.

The pattern

What the winning ideas have in common

The hardware lists differ. The shape of the work does not. A judge can retell each project in one breath, and a visitor at the HKCEC booth can see what to do.

  1. 1
    A local problem with a person in it

    A driver circling for a meter. A senior whose grip is fading. A student under a heavy bag. A tree in typhoon wind. The problem is already in the city. The team’s job is to show why it matters, with observation, interviews, or a count.

  2. 2
    A signal the board can read

    A camera and a plate. Pressure in a ball. Posture in a frame. Tilt and wind. micro:bit is the messenger, sometimes the measurer. The invention is the link, the rule, and the way a person meets the result.

  3. 3
    An action, timed to the moment

    Pay through a system people already use. Keep squeezing because a character climbs. Warn with light and sound the right adults can hear. Flag a tree before it fails. Information that sits in a spreadsheet does not score the way an action does.

  4. 4
    A second try after the first one fails

    The gold team’s camera missed plates, so they added another way to identify the car and rebuilt the model more than once. That trail is the design mark. A single polished demo, with no record of what changed, leaves the largest primary mark — creativity — thin.

For this team

Design is the part you can win on purpose.

Primary creativity is 30% of the mark, the largest slice. It asks for an original approach, a look at other solutions, and a join between fields. Interface design, a game loop, and a sensor reading are three fields. Joined around one Hong Kong user, they are a project.

Vibe coding — building the app or game quickly with coding tools, then judging it by letting people use it — fits the validation mark, on one condition. The team can explain every screen, and a test log shows what users forced them to change. A beautiful file with no users is a poster.

HKIF’s own aims sit in the same place: meet people outside school, put the idea in front of the public, and carry a clear story to an overseas programme. A booth a stranger understands in a minute is ready for that.

Primary division · last year’s Fair

Four projects, and the move inside each

Gold, silver, and bronze below follow the project notes for this briefing. TreeWatch is the primary Most Popular Award, chosen by public vote at the fair in June 2026. Chinese names are given where a published title is on record.

Park & Easy Toll 街泊路路通

#2509 · Tin Shui Wai Methodist Primary School · Teacher: Chan Chun Wai
Li Shing Yurk, Chen Chak Yu, Moo Pak Hang, So Ka Man

Gold

Essence. A metered bay should turn over. The longer a car stays, the more it should cost, and the driver should pay through a system they already have, without hunting for coins or an app they have never opened.

What they built

An AI camera reads the plate and the start time. A micro:bit sends that to the driver’s phone over Bluetooth. On departure the fee is worked out on a rising scale, and the driver is sent to HKeToll to pay.

What coverage adds

The work started from a parking dispute on Heung Wo Street in Tsuen Wan. The team interviewed drivers and parents. When the camera missed plates in poor light and on sideways bays, they added an NFC tap, modelled on a student card, and rebuilt the model about three times.

Why it scores

Needs analysis is a street and a set of conversations. Creativity is the rising fee plus a public payment rail, after a backup method earned its place by a failed test. Impact is a bay freed for the next short stay.

Take this, leave the parking meter

Start from a scene you witnessed. When the sensor fails, add a second method and write down why. Use a service people already trust. The screen that explains the fee in one glance is the piece a design-strong team would have made sharper.

Grip the Health

#2370 · CCC Chun Kwong Primary School · Teacher: Chan Yun Lam
Chan Oi Ki, To Sheung, Wong Chi Shing Bryant, Lo Yan To

Silver

Essence. Grip exercise is medicine people skip. Make a character climb only while the hand squeezes, and the exercise becomes something a senior wants to repeat.

What they built

A pressure sensor inside a grip ball, a micro:bit, and a Scratch app. Two modes: Grip Test and Grip Training. Squeeze and the character rises. Release and it descends. The aim is regular practice, against sarcopenia and the falls that follow.

Why it scores

The sensor is ordinary. The loop is the idea: measurement and motivation in one object, aimed at a named health risk. It joins physical therapy, a game, and a board. That is the creativity criterion in primary colours.

Take this

A game earns the mark when the body action is the input, and when a real user comes back to it. Two modes show they understood a person (test, then train), not only a component.

Leave this lane

Another grip ball, another “health game” that claims to diagnose, or a Scratch sprite with no session log. Our students can build a finer interface than Scratch. The fair still wants the squeeze — or whatever the action is — to be physically real, and the before-and-after to come from people.

Schoolbag Monitor

#2590 · SKH Tsing Yi Estate Ho Chak Wan Primary School · Teacher: Chen Yinghua
Yeung Cheuk Him, Lee Chak Yin, Fung Hei Ching, Lau Chung Young

Bronze

Essence. A heavy bag is a health problem you can see badly with one glance. Read posture and the body’s pivot points as well as the picture, then tell the student, the teacher, and the parent in light and sound.

What they built

A camera the student walks past. Detection combines posture, pivot points, and image analysis, because a bag’s outline alone is a weak clue. LEDs and an audio alert follow the result.

Why it scores

Creativity here is the method: they say why a single model fails, and they add a second way of looking. Impact depends on the alert reaching someone who can lighten the bag tomorrow. The accuracy claim only holds if they counted hits and misses.

Take this

If a reading is unreliable, show the failure, then add one more signal and publish the error count. Design the alert for the person who can act, in a form they notice without panic.

Leave this lane

A camera on classmates to score posture, mood, or “bad behaviour.” Consent, accuracy, and privacy all get harder, and the shape of the project is one the judges have just seen.

TreeWatch 弱樹臨風警報器

#2995 · Diocesan Boys’ School Primary Division · Teacher: Lam Tsz Kin Felix
Yu Lok Chee Clarence, Chan Alexis Chun Ming, Chan Adam Cheuk Wang, Lee Quentin Sung Hei

Most popular

Essence. In a typhoon city, people worry about trees falling. Two simple numbers — how far the trunk leans, how hard the wind pushes — watched over time, can speak before the dangerous hour.

What they built

Tilt and wind sensors on a compact micro:bit. Continuous logging, a stability judgement, and an early warning. The public voted it the primary Most Popular Award. The model is easy to point at and easy to retell.

Why people stopped

Familiar risk, visible hardware, a warning with a purpose. Popularity at this fair is a presentation lesson: a stranger can explain the danger and the response after one minute at the booth. micro:bit was enough.

Take this

Make the risk readable in one look. Words and icons together, a threshold written in plain language, a reason the number matters this season in Hong Kong. That is interface work.

Leave this lane

Another tilt-and-wind tree alarm, or a generic “smart sensor dashboard” with six charts and no decision. The vote went to a warning someone could act on.

Fit

Where UI, games, micro:bit, and Arduino actually help

The board is the witness. The interface is the argument.

  • User research is already a design skill. Interviews, a short survey, and watching someone fail a task are the 20% needs-analysis mark. Write the problem in the user’s words.
  • The app or game is where you outbuild a Scratch sprite. Empty states, a wrong-button undo, type large enough for a grandparent, colour plus a word so the meaning survives colour-blindness, a screen that reads from across a playground. Judges feel this before they read the board.
  • Keep the hardware thin so the demo survives the hall. One sensor, one cable for the booth, a labelled recording from the school test if the hall radio is noisy. Say when a run is recorded.
  • micro:bit fits radio between boards, the v2 microphone, buttons, Bluetooth to a phone, and simple logging. Arduino fits temperature probes, humidity, CO2 modules, a load cell, and large arcade buttons.
  • Everyone on the team of three or four can tell the failure. Judges ask the quiet student what broke and what you changed.
A trap with the micro:bit temperature block. The on-board reading is the chip, warmed by the processor, not the room and not the playground. Heat and air projects need an external sensor (a DHT22, a BME280, a CO2 module). State that in the report. Honesty about the instrument is a validation point.

How the mark scheme reads this kind of team

Same five questions in every division. The weights shift. Primary pays the most for creativity. Senior pays more for proof, scale, and a research trail. Testing and Certification is a separate prize for secondary teams only.

Outstanding Research Project Awards

Senior secondary only, and only among shortlisted teams. Up to three teams. The extra mark is commitment over time (20%), repeated testing and improvement (30%), learning from failure (20%), and a full documented journey with sources and evidence (30%). A vibe-coded interface can win this only if the notebook is as real as the screens: dates, failed builds, the change each failure caused.

Testing and Certification Special Awards

Junior and senior together, judged by the Hong Kong Council for Testing and Certification. One gold, one silver, one bronze. Relevance to testing (30%), feasibility and standards (25%), scientific merit and a look at similar products (20%), originality (15%), presentation (10%). Primary teams are outside this prize. They can still do the science. Two ideas below — Heat Whistle and Lunchbox Line — are the ones that can grow into this award if the team is secondary, because each can name a public method and compare two instruments.

Directions

Six ideas that use design as the invention

Each one leaves the four winning lanes, stays buildable on micro:bit or Arduino, and gives the interface a job. Filter by the kind of work the team enjoys. Any of them can be told at primary depth, then deepened with more trials if the team is secondary.

01

Class Breath 課室一口風

For one classroom that goes stale behind shut windows, and the teacher who decides when to air it.

GameArduinoAir

Hong Kong classrooms often stay sealed against noise and heat. Students get sleepy. Nobody shares a sign that it is time to open a window. The project is a class creature the students design. It slumps when the air is stale and recovers when the room is aired. The score is the room’s, never a named child’s.

Signal

Arduino and a CO2 module (SCD41 or similar). If the school cannot get one, use a BME280 for temperature and humidity and narrow the claim to comfort. Say so on the board. A spot reading is a sample, not an 8-hour certification test.

The screen

One full-screen creature, named by the class, with states students illustrate: awake, drowsy, asking for a window. A second view for the teacher is a single line of the last hour. Large type. No leaderboard.

Research to do first

One lesson of observation (window state, how the room feels). A short class survey. A talk with the teacher who runs the air-conditioner. Cite the EPD Indoor Air Quality objectives: 8-hour CO2 of 800 ppmv (Excellent) and 1,000 ppmv (Good) for offices and public places, and explain that your lesson sample is shorter than that test.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A scolding alarm. A chart that ranks students. Both punish. The creature asks the class to act together, which is the Grip the Health loop pointed at shared air.

Three tests

Three afternoons in the same room. Log the reading, whether the window opened, and a one-tap “how sleepy” from the class. Change the threshold or the creature because of what happened. The graph of the three days is the validation page.

Impact you can claim

This class aired the room when the creature asked, and the reading moved. A primary team stops there. A secondary team can repeat across two rooms and compare with the published 8-hour objectives without calling the result a certificate.

The question a judge will ask “Did the creature change what the class did, or only decorate a sensor?” Answer with the afternoon the window stayed shut, the afternoon it opened, and the two readings.

02

Heat Whistle 熱哨

For the PE teacher on the playground, and in a second mode, children waiting in the sun at the bus bay.

StandardArduinoTwo modes

Heat and humidity in Hong Kong make “stop, drink, move to shade” a real decision. It is often made by feel. The project gives the teacher a number they can explain, on a scoreboard readable from across the court. The same sensor, second screen: a calm clock for the bus bay that says how long a child has been in full sun and points them to the canopy.

Signal

Arduino with a DHT22 or BME280 in a shaded housing. Sun on the sensor invents a hotter day. Write that design choice on the board. The micro:bit can be the remote display. The measurement stays on the external sensor.

The screen

A sports scoreboard: huge type, an icon plus a word (colour alone fails some viewers), three messages such as “keep going,” “water break,” “move the drill to shade.” Bus-bay mode is a separate layout, illustrated, plain, and free of scare language. Two modes, like Grip Test and Grip Training, show two users of one instrument.

Research to do first

Interview two PE teachers. Read the current Hong Kong Observatory page for Hot and Very Hot Weather Warnings and quote its trigger. Log a week of playground readings with a household thermometer before the prototype exists. That week is needs analysis.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A phone notification the teacher will not see mid-game. A medical “heat stroke detector.” You are building a decision aid with a rule you can read aloud, tied to Observatory guidance, not a diagnosis.

Three tests

Can the teacher read the message at 5 metres and at 10? On which days did the badge agree with the Observatory warning, and on which days did the teacher disagree? Change the threshold because of those disagreements, and keep the disagreement log. Compare the probe with a second thermometer and write down the gap.

If the team is secondary

This is a Testing and Certification path. Name the method, compare instruments, and describe what the school tool is and is not. Wider use is a PE panel across one school, then a written note on what would have to be true to share it. Primary teams do the same tests and present them as proof the tool works.

The question a judge will ask “Why should a teacher trust this more than looking at the children?” Answer with the shaded sensor, the second thermometer, and a day the number and the teacher disagreed — and what you changed.

03

Three Buttons 三個掣

For a senior the school already visits, and the family member who wants a simple piece of news.

UX researchArduino or micro:bit

Many products for older people fail at the screen, not at the feature list. This project starts by watching a senior stop. The result is a box with three enormous buttons — “I’m home,” “Please call,” “What’s today” — and a family page with three lines, a time, and a way to undo a mis-press. Cantonese first. One task per view.

Signal

Three arcade buttons on Arduino or a micro:bit, plus a buzzer so the box itself confirms the press. The event is the signal. There is no health sensor and no claim about illness. Grip the Health measured the body. This one removes steps.

The screen

The senior side is the box, not a display. The family side is a page: huge type, empty state (“no news yet today”), timestamp, and “that was the wrong button.” Students design it with the family, on paper first. The fair demo can be the box and a laptop on the same table.

Research to do first

Visit. Watch one senior try a normal phone task and write the exact step they abandon. Ask the family which three messages would matter this week. Consent from the senior and the family, in writing the school already uses for visits. Six people is a small test. Call it that.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A simplified phone skin. Voice only. Run the same three tasks on those and on the box. Keep whichever wins on errors and time, even if the box loses and you have to change it. The comparison is the creativity mark.

Three tests

Same tasks, same people, version by version. Record time, mis-presses, and one sentence from each person. Version 2 exists because of a sentence (“I didn’t know if it heard me” → the buzzer). Version 3 is the one on the table.

Impact you can claim

These seniors finished a message their family could read. You are not claiming a product for every household in Hong Kong. A senior team that wants scale writes what would have to change to leave this centre: power, language, who maintains the box.

The question a judge will ask “Why buttons, when phones already exist?” Answer with the step where the phone lost, the task times, and the undo.

04

Quiet Atlas 靜音地圖

For students who need a calmer place to read, including classmates who are easily overwhelmed, and for the librarian.

Mapmicro:bit v2

“Be quiet” is a slogan. Noise moves through a school day. The project is a floor plan, drawn and labelled in the words students use, coloured and patterned by sound level at three times of day, with a card that says where to sit now.

Signal

One micro:bit v2 microphone. Walk a fixed route, or leave the board in four agreed spots. Write down place, time, and level. The map is drawn from the sheet. Radio between boards is optional and easy to drop if the corridor is hostile to it.

The screen

This is the design piece. A plan of one floor, pattern plus colour (not colour alone), room names students recognise, a “sit here now” card. Print the same visual system on the display board so the booth and the map are one product. Three drawn versions: the first was confusing, the labels changed, the third is the one the reading group used.

Research to do first

Ask the librarian and two students where they go when it is too loud. Walk with them. The map should answer their sentence, not a generic noise poster.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A siren that shames a noisy class. A league table of the loudest room. The atlas informs a choice of seat. Shaming is a different product, and a worse one.

Three tests

The map predicts the calmest spot at lunch. Measure again and mark hits and misses. The reading group uses it for several days and says whether they moved. Fix the label or the route because someone misread a room.

Impact you can claim

A named group found a seat that measured quieter, and could show you where on the plan. Presentation marks come almost free if the drawing is clear — and only if the measurements are on the board beside it.

The question a judge will ask “Is this a poster or a measurement?” Point to the route, the three times of day, the second measurement, and the seat the group actually chose.

05

Lunchbox Line 飯盒線

For families who pack lunch in the morning and are unsure what the Hong Kong heat does to it before noon.

StandardArduinoT&C path

A single horizontal line, morning to lunch, with the measured temperature of a dummy box drawn on it. The Centre for Food Safety danger band, above 4°C and below 60°C, is marked on the same line. A parent should grasp it in a few seconds. The sentence under the line is written with parents, then rewritten when they misread it.

Signal

Arduino and a waterproof DS18B20 in a closed dummy box (water or rice the team does not eat). Log every few minutes from arrival until lunch, once on a hotter day and once on a milder day.

The screen

One line, one band, one sentence. Time on the axis, temperature as position, the danger band labelled in words. No gauge cluster. Test the sentence with two parents: “your rice entered the warm band at 10:40” either lands or it doesn’t. Change the words.

Research to do first

A short parent survey: when is food packed, and how long is it out of the fridge? Read the CFS guidance and cite it. The danger zone they publish is 4°C to 60°C, with a 2-hour / 4-hour rule for high-risk food. Quote the booklet. Do not invent a “safe number of hours” of your own.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A scare alert every minute. An AI camera pointed at real lunches. The line is calmer and matches the evidence you have, which is temperature over time.

Three tests

Calibrate: ice water and a warm cup, your probe beside a kitchen thermometer, the difference written down. Then the two school days. Then the parent sentence test. That calibration note is the project’s scientific spine.

If the team is secondary

Testing and Certification judges look for exactly this: a public method, a reference instrument, repeated trials, and a clear limit on the claim. The tool informs a family. It does not certify a lunchbox. Primary teams keep the same honesty and skip the special-award framing.

The question a judge will ask “Are you telling parents to throw food away?” Answer: we show when the dummy box entered the band CFS describes, and we tell families to read that guidance. The probe was checked against another thermometer. The gap was this many degrees.

06

Your Turn 輪到你

For classmates who sit out group games because the rules are shouted and fast.

Gamemicro:bit radio

Watch one recess and count turns. Some children rarely get one. The project is a set of micro:bits, one per player, that light and sound for the person whose turn it is, plus a game on the class screen that only moves when the lit board is pressed. The interface is the inclusion.

Signal

Four to six micro:bits on radio. One bridge board on USB talks to the game, so the booth has a cable even if radio misbehaves. A teacher remote can pause or pass a turn. The press on the lit board is the only input that counts.

The screen

A game the team vibe-codes: big icons, a practice round, a way to leave without being displayed as “out.” Design the pause and the mis-press with the classmates who usually wait. Speed is a setting, because the first version will be too fast. That complaint is a feature of the log.

Research to do first

One recess tally: how many turns named classmates get in the existing game. A conversation with them and with the PE teacher about what “fair” feels like. Consent before any recording. A tally sheet is enough.

Alternatives to show you rejected

A faster reaction game that rewards the quickest hand. A scoreboard of winners. Both recreate the exclusion. The turn light gives the slower player the same invitation.

Three tests

Play the old game and the new one. Count turns again. Ask the students who used to wait whether they would play tomorrow. Slow the timing because they asked. Bring both tallies.

Impact you can claim

In this group, turns spread. You can show the counts. A secondary extension is a second class and a note on radio range in a real hall — a practical limit, which judges trust more than a promise to “roll out to all schools.”

The question a judge will ask “Could you not just take turns without a gadget?” Answer with the tally from the shouted game, then the tally with the light. The gadget is there because the count changed.

Shorter starts, if none of the six fit the term

What we left behind. A canteen scale (load cell and Arduino) and a weekly poster the team designs, in kilograms of leftovers. Talk to the kitchen staff first. Skip photographs of students’ plates.

West-facing glare. A light sensor and a “lower the blind” card for one room, only after observation shows glare actually stops work. Otherwise it is a gadget on a window.

Movement between lessons. A micro:bit step game on the stairs. It earns a place only if a PE teacher or nurse confirms students skip movement. Otherwise Heat Whistle and Your Turn carry more need.

Same path for every idea

How to build so the criteria can see the work

1

Problem, in someone’s words

A scene, a count, and at least one conversation or a short questionnaire. Photographs of places are fine. Photographs of people need consent. List every source, including pages, libraries, icon packs, and the coding tools you used. The rules require that, and they forbid other people’s IP in the project.

2

Two rejected ideas

A page with the approaches you did not build, and the reason — a user disliked it, a sensor lied, it copied a recent winner, it shamed someone. This page is how primary creativity (30%) becomes visible.

3

Version one, on a cable

One signal into a page the team can vibe-code. Ugly is allowed. The micro:bit or Arduino should be explained by any student: what is read, what is sent, what the screen does with it.

4

A test that hurts a little

Users miss a button, a teacher cannot read the type, a probe disagrees with a thermometer, the creature nags. Write the result down the same day. Change one thing.

5

Version two and three

Each version has a date, a photo of the screen, and the sentence that forced the change. Three versions is the trail the gold parking team could point to. It is also what Outstanding Research, later, calls repeated validation.

6

One booth, one story

Board order follows the mark scheme: problem and evidence, ideas you dropped, photos of the three versions, the numbers, who it helps. Same colours as the app. A sixty-second talk: we saw this, we tried that, it failed because, we changed it, here is the number. Live demo, plus a labelled recording.

Protect the entry

Leave these lanes alone

The four projects already on the table

A parking meter with plate recognition, a grip-strength ball, a backpack camera, a tree tilt-and-wind alarm. Judges saw them in 2025–26. Use their shape. Build a different problem.

Diagnosis and surveillance

Claims to detect sarcopenia, depression, food poisoning, or “bad behaviour.” Cameras on classmates. Public rankings of who drank less or whose bag looks heavy. Measure a condition of a place or a task, inform an adult, and keep the person’s dignity in the interface.

A dashboard with nobody in it

Six charts, no decision, no user test. Vibe coding makes this the easiest thing to generate and the easiest thing to see through. One screen, one action, one log of people using it.

The on-chip temperature, and a wireless-only demo

micro:bit’s built-in temperature is the processor. A Bluetooth demo with no cable will fail on a noisy fair floor. Measure with the right sensor. Present with a cable. Label any recorded run.
